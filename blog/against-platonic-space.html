Against Platonic Space: The Externalization of Intelligibility

Petri Autio
November 18, 2026

Scientific boundaries have become wonderfully porous. Biology now speaks to mathematics, computer science to cognition, and some of the more adventurous investigators sail between them without waiting for permission from the departments concerned. Michael Levin is among the boldest of these voyagers. At Tufts University his laboratory studies developmental bioelectricity, morphogenesis, regeneration and cognition in cellular collectives. Its work on Xenobots and Anthrobots makes especially vivid how cells can acquire capacities their familiar arrangements never display. Levin has carried these discoveries into an experimentally motivated Platonism, intent on overcoming physical reductionism without confining himself to immutable Forms.

In A Short Argument on Platonic Space (31 March 2026), Levin asserts that “There are facts which are not physical facts.” He points to prime numbers, topology, and mathematical relations that changing the universe's physical constants would leave intact. So far, so good. But a distinction between mathematical and physical knowledge cannot by itself establish two separately existing realms.

Levin's challenge to reductionism deserves an answer that does not return biology to an inventory of mute physical parts. His interactionism is explicit, provisional and experimentally ambitious; he even allows that the two spaces might eventually prove to be aspects of a deeper reality. The difficulty lies deeper than the number of realms. If Platonic Space is a useful way to investigate forms, its value is measured by what it reveals. If patterns supply causal input from elsewhere, we must understand how the purported source, the embodiment and their relation are determined. The old separation of thought and being, long subjected to immanent criticism, has returned with an ingenious laboratory apparatus. Let us see whether the apparatus can sustain the separation.

The abstract universal: what does the common name ‘pattern’ explain?

In A Short Argument, and more extensively in Ingressing Minds, Levin groups mathematics, dynamical systems, organisms and minds as patterns belonging to a structured space of possibilities. He proposes an empirically investigable continuum of agency, ranging from mathematical invariants to minds capable of learning and revising their goals. Yet the common designation 'pattern' is still an abstract universal. A concrete universal would have to show how its own differentiations arise: why invariance, living self-maintenance and rational self-determination are not interchangeable instances of the same general feature. A scale of competence can measure differences; it cannot by itself derive the determinate forms it measures. [1, 2]

A mathematical constant does not preserve itself against a hostile environment. A living organism does: it makes its surroundings into conditions of its continued existence, and its organs have their functions only within that self-maintaining activity. A thinking subject can go further, examining and transforming the ends by which it acts. These are not simply increasing quantities of one property called agency. The universal becomes concrete only insofar as we comprehend these differences and their transitions, rather than arranging finished specimens along a line.

Levin's more radical claim goes beyond classification. In §6.4 of Ingressing Minds, a pattern may be described as the agent and the embodied machine as its scratchpad; he also allows the division between machine and data to shift with the observer. This perspectival reversibility is instructive, but his further assignment of priority to the pattern does not follow from it. The observer can choose which relations to foreground without choosing which of the terms exists independently. A mathematical pattern might be studied apart from this machine; the actual agent's powers still require the determinate organization through which it acts. [2, §6.4]

Mathematical necessity does not establish ontological independence

Levin begins with an observation I readily accept; mathematical truths do not depend on the particular physical constants of our universe. It would be absurd to suppose that changing the mass of an electron could change the value of e. But what, precisely, follows from this?

Levin declares: “If you agree that you can’t simply dissolve the math department, then that’s it – we have 2 distinct (but interacting) realms.” In a later reply he makes the choice stark: “Unless you have a rebuttal of how to reduce mathematical objects to facts of physics, we must adopt a Platonic-like view.” The same sentence proceeds to reject physicalism as factually incorrect. [4] The dichotomy remains false. The necessity of mathematics cannot be manufactured by altering physical constants; neither must its objects therefore become separately acting causes. The determinate relation between mathematical form and physical actuality is precisely what the choice between reduction and a second realm leaves unexamined.

Levin can reply that the word 'realm' is optional. He expressly sets aside the question whether Platonic Space is ontologically real and, in a footnote to Ingressing Minds, declines to distinguish fully its status as metaphysical entity from epistemic framework. Even his suggestion that mathematics might be folded into an enlarged physics preserves two distinguishable domains of inquiry. Fair enough. What cannot remain merely verbal is the claim of information or causal input *from* an independently constituted space. If no extra input is meant, the second address contributes no ontological explanation; if it is meant, the distinction must make a discoverable difference. [1; 2, §1(e), n. 1]

Mathematical form can be understood without pointing to one particular empirical instance. That independence of a particular instance does not make the form a self-sufficient being, nor reduce its necessity to the physical circumstances in which we encounter it. An unbuilt bridge can be mathematically described; the possible structure has objective determinations. But those determinations do not assemble steel, and the steel does not manufacture mathematical necessity. We must understand the determinate unity of form and actuality without assigning either a separate residence and subsequently inventing a passage between them. Levin's two addresses turn a distinction within intelligibility into a transaction between supposed occupants.

When mathematics becomes causal

Once mathematics has been assigned a distinct domain, Levin asks that it do explanatory work: “if they were different, facts in the physical world would be different.” This is not a proposal that numbers shove atoms about. It is an argument from mathematical constraint and counterfactual dependence to a distinct kind of causation. Its force deserves examination, since the leap from formal necessity to independent cause supports the rest of the architecture. [1]

In his example, a different distribution of primes would have altered the familiar 13- and 17-year cycles of periodical cicadas. This is a serious point about explanatory relevance. The mathematics helps specify which periodicities could reduce encounters with predators or competing cycles. Levin also argues that mathematical relations can enable biological capacities, rather than merely constrain them. We should grant both claims without smuggling in the conclusion about an independent causal realm.

The counterfactual concerning primes is not an ordinary intervention of the sort contemplated in Pearl's causal modelling. Levin himself acknowledges that nothing physical can change their distribution, while his engineer's notion of cause appeals to what one can “intervene on, understand, and exploit”. His additional criterion treats the most illuminating reason for research as causal. Such explanatory relevance is genuine, but depends partly on the question the investigator asks. It does not establish an additional causal agent. Levin recognizes that the term 'cause' may need revision and defers a fuller account to another paper. The inferential burden remains: what turns the mathematical determination of possible periodicities into an input from a separately constituted domain? [1; 2, §1, second lettered list (c); 4]

Arithmetic enters the account of cicada periodicity through the concrete relations of life cycles, predator pressures and possible synchronization. Its necessity does not show that the numbers are independently operating biological agents. One can well honour mathematical objectivity without imagining an extra causal transaction.

Levin's Pythagorean reply is a parity argument: if mathematics constrains physics, why deny interaction between the physical and non-physical? Because a constraint is not yet an independent cause. More fundamentally, cause and effect are themselves abstractions when treated as finished things confronting one another. A cause is actual in its effect; what appears as an effect enters into further causal relations, and reciprocal action reveals that neither term is fully intelligible in isolation. The mathematical articulation of physical possibility belongs to this intelligible whole. It does not establish an additional agent standing outside the process and dispatching its form inward.

Where did the Xenobots come from?

Xenobots built from frog cells and Anthrobots assembled from human cells have exhibited capacities absent from their familiar anatomical arrangements. Anthrobots have aided neural wound closure in experimental settings. Xenobots, for their part, have shown unexpected collective behaviours. Their precise engineered configurations lack an evolutionary history of selection. Levin properly refuses to let 'emergence' serve as a congratulatory name for our surprise. He notes that no one predicted the new transcriptomic and behavioural features in advance. A genealogy supplied after the fact is not yet a predictive explanation. [1, 2]

Cellular history still matters. The constituent cells inherit bioelectric, biochemical and developmental competencies, whose new interrelations alter what the collective can do. Yet this answer must not become the very retrospective story Levin criticizes. The task is prospective: predict how changes in coupling, geometry, tissue history and boundary conditions alter the repertoire of attainable forms. If those predictions fail, investigate why; do not merely invoke 'emergence' with greater solemnity.

Levin explicitly rejects the inference that Anthrobots alone establish Platonic ontology. He also insists upon a second experimental pillar: minimal computational systems whose components are fully specified. Their sorting, de-sorting and clustering behaviours deprive us of any easy appeal to the evolutionary history of cells. The stronger demand is for a prospective account of these competencies, rather than a retrospective label. [2, §§5.5, 6.3, 7.3]

Mapping developmental possibility spaces could transform synthetic biology and the design of minimal agents. Even a metaphysics that eventually fails might encourage excellent experiments; a laboratory need not demand philosophical perfection as an entrance fee. But the claim that a space of possibilities supplies causal power from beyond embodiment incurs a different obligation. Predicting what a system can do by exploring its affordances does not establish which ontology made that prediction true.

The risk is that genesis quietly becomes retrieval. In §5.3 of his longer paper Levin explicitly treats the complexity of form as pointed to by a seed and retrieved across a pattern space and its physical interface, rather than constructed through an embodied developmental process. As a research metaphor, retrieval encourages the search for recurrent possibilities. As an account of origin, it still has to explain why this configuration, with these histories and constraints, realizes this capacity and another does not. Its work remains to be done exactly where a theory of immanent organization begins. [2, §5.3]

The thin client and its mysterious server

The shift from genesis to retrieval becomes clearest in Levin's technological analogy. If an organism draws its capacities from a structured space, its body resembles a terminal accessing resources elsewhere. In the short essay he calls this a thin client; in the longer paper he speaks of pointers and interfaces. The image captures something real: an experimenter may build a modest arrangement and obtain far more than the initial design described. Whether the additional competence was already there as an independent pattern or became actual through the arrangement is precisely the question the metaphor cannot decide. [1; 2, §5.3]

Levin is explicit about the pointer's attractions in §5.3 of his longer paper: a modest interface can yield surprisingly rich behaviour, its mapping to an outcome can be nonlinear, and exploiting that mapping may require investigation of a wider possibility space. He also writes, “no doubt the pointer metaphor will fail at some point.” Agreed. My objection is directed at what the metaphor has yet to establish: the separate existence of the space to which the pointer supposedly points. [2, §5.3]

Here the pointer becomes philosophically instructive. In computing, it operates within an already constituted arrangement of memory, addressing, execution and rules of access. These relations make the pointer possible in the first place. An embryo's supposed access to non-physical patterns similarly calls for an account of the relation that constitutes both interface and address space. Without it, the analogy redescribes the mystery as a successful connection to a server. [2]

This is no reason to prohibit Levin's experiments. Quite the contrary: as the mappings become experimentally determinate, they may reveal whether the relevant competencies are constituted through the system's organization or whether some further, independently identifiable contribution is required.

The minimal computational systems make the issue unusually clean. In §5.5 Levin champions models whose components can be specified and inspected; §6.3 describes sorting algorithms that temporarily decrease sortedness when swaps are blocked and distributed algorithms that cluster by type. He readily grants that such systems may be deterministic. His question is why these particular rules generate such unanticipated, mathematically structured competencies. We should not answer him by repeating that the code is visible: that is already his premise. Rather, the complete transition rules and conditions produce the clustering on each relevant run. Where the side effect requires no additional computation, there is correspondingly no demonstrated additional computational input. Its mathematical intelligibility does not promote the relevant mathematics into a separately acting cause; that is the inference rejected earlier. The positive task is to derive in advance which rule changes and perturbations yield the observed repertoire. [2, §§5.5, 6.3, 7.3]

Levin allows reciprocal interaction: patterns may affect embodiments and be modified by them. The fuller statements occur in his discussion of mutable forms and the open questions at the end of the paper, while §1 calls the interaction 'likely, bi-directional'. This is more than a concession that two finished entities can influence each other. If embodiment changes the determination of a pattern, and the pattern changes the determination of embodiment, their identities cannot simply be assumed as complete before their encounter. Reciprocal action is precisely where the supposed independence of cause and effect becomes unstable. The task is to comprehend the unity that differentiates and relates them, not to add a communication channel between two independently furnished worlds. [1; 2, §1, first lettered list (g), second lettered list (b), §7.3]

The problem of mutable patterns

“Are the patterns unchanging and eternal? I don’t commit to that.” — Michael Levin, A Short Argument on Platonic Space

Levin's strongest reply distinguishes the kind from its occurrence. Figure 10C and the closing discussion of Ingressing Minds suggest that a kind of mind may be available beforehand, while an individual instance emerges and is moulded by embodied experience. But a universal kind does not act as an individual. Nor is the individual a merely accidental container for an already complete universal. The living particular gives the universal determinate actuality; in turn its activity is intelligible through the kind of whole it constitutes. What must be explained is this becoming-individual, not a pre-existing mind selecting a temporary scratchpad. [2, Fig. 10C, §7.3]

The static and the mutable must be kept apart. The value of e raises the question of whether mathematical necessity entails an independently existing cause. A developing mind raises another: what individuates the supposed pattern through changes that partly constitute it? We gain nothing by forcing both cases into a single refutation.

One might insist that the general kind remains independently real and that the individual merely instantiates it. But instantiation is precisely the relation requiring explanation. An abstract type cannot determine which individual exists, what it does, or how it changes merely by remaining abstract. The organism's self-maintenance, its differentiation of parts and environment, and its capacity to transform its own conditions are not external decorations of agency. They are determinations of the agent itself. Where Levin gives the pattern priority over its embodied 'scratchpad', he mistakes a moment abstracted from the living whole for that whole's source.

Can the research programme save the metaphysics?

Levin's pragmatism is more formidable than an embarrassed retreat from ontology. Section 1 and footnote 7 explicitly allow intermediate views rejecting reductionism while retaining monism; he argues that quarrels over realms have no practical consequence and that the stronger framework is valuable because it may uncover enablements neglected by familiar approaches. In his 11 November 2025 symposium reply he even allows the two spaces to become aspects of a deeper reality. I grant the methodological freedom. But treating provisional dualism as an innocent diagram is difficult when the diagram instructs us to search for an extra input and to interpret novel competence as retrieval. A representation becomes scientifically consequential through the variables it directs us to measure, the mechanisms it excludes from view, and the explanations it licenses. [2, §1, n. 7; 4]

Levin supplies a more specific experimental wager than an appeal to useful metaphors. In §7.3 he expects predictions that break with conventional computational frameworks; in his later discussions he proposes measurable computation beyond standard physical accounting, potentially calling for revised thermodynamic bookkeeping, including Landauer-style bounds. This is an intelligible rival claim, not a prediction we should pretend he lacks. The critical test requires a jointly specified accounting baseline for the full implemented transition structure, state preparation, measurement, information and energy costs. Reproducible computational input beyond that baseline, predicted in advance by an ingress model and not recoverable by a corrected organizational accounting, would favour Levin. A discrepancy against a selectively impoverished baseline would not. Levin also frames programme failure conjunctively: no quantifiable extra contribution and no novel discoveries or engineering capacities. The latter criterion tests fertility; the former bears on his causal hypothesis. [2, §1, first lettered list (i), §7.3]

The 3 January sorting-and-clustering example sharpens the point. Levin suggests the clustering is obtained as a free second service alongside sorting. Very well: if no additional computational work was required, there is no additional work whose origin needs a metaphysical supplier. The same implemented dynamics generated both effects. His genuinely distinct forecast would be a reproducible computational contribution beyond a complete, prospectively agreed resource tally, not merely another competence absent from the designer's description. Even there, a revised accounting and an extra-physical input are competing explanations, and the test must distinguish them before declaring a winner. [2, §§6.3, 7.3; 4]

Levin's opposition between brute facts that happen to hold and a separately existing Platonic domain overlooks a third possibility. Objective possibility belongs to the determinate intelligibility of actuality: its conditions, internal relations, exclusions and powers of development. This is neither a heap of physical occurrences nor a second population of mathematical beings. Possibilities can be unrealized and nevertheless necessary or intelligible; a mathematical structure is not exhausted by any one instance. But the necessity of an abstraction and the self-determination of a concrete whole are different logical achievements. Levin's four operational senses of 'real' can guide inquiry here. They do not settle whether form is an independent cause. [2, §4.2]

Footnote 7 asks what practical difference a non-reductive monist alternative makes, points to the time such views have had to produce scientific advances, and challenges rivals to produce their own roadmaps. I take the challenge. A philosophical account of immanent form has not already earned a laboratory track record merely by being philosophically compelling. Nor is Levin alone in proposing to vary coupling, rules or boundaries; those interventions are part of his programme too. The distinctive proposal is to preregister how changing concrete organization transforms the *space of reachable competencies*, then derive which apparently novel behaviours follow from the revised transition structure and its cost. Compare that prospective organizational forecast with an ingress model specifying a contribution beyond a shared computational or biological baseline. The models may recommend identical experiments, and in that case any discovery belongs to the common empirical programme, not uniquely to Platonic Space. Only divergent, successful predictions could begin to vindicate its distinctive causal story. [2, §1, n. 7, §§4.2, 5.5, 7.3] The table makes the differences in inferential commitment explicit.

The intelligibility of actuality

An immanent science of form does not begin with a finished physical mechanism and attach a mathematical description afterward. It investigates how a whole determines its own parts and conditions while existing only through their activity. In a living collective, the distinction between system and environment is itself produced and maintained through regulation; its boundary is a result of activity as well as a condition of it. Change bioelectric connectivity or tissue organization, and one may alter not merely an outcome but what the collective can sustain as a goal. In minimal algorithms, rules and their concrete execution similarly determine a field of possible transitions, though computation alone should not be confused with living self-relation. The possibility space is objective because its determinations can be understood, tested and developed; it is not a ghostly cause standing behind them.

As we flush Levin's Platonic Spaces down the drain, consider the whirlpool that remains. Its spiral can be described mathematically whether or not this particular vortex exists. In the water before us, however, the spiral is actual only through the movement of water, pressure and boundary. The form is neither an alien visitor nor an arbitrary name for the moving liquid: it is the intelligible determination of that movement. Alter the relations and this vortex changes or disappears. Levin may call the drain a pointer to a space of vortices. But the pointer cannot explain why this vortex exists, since the concrete movement that constitutes it is precisely what the analogy leaves to be understood.

Now consider a salamander regrowing a limb. The whirlpool maintains a form through moving matter, but it does not repair a missing part according to the requirements of its own continued life. The salamander's wound reorganizes relations among cells, tissues, signals and the whole organism; a limb is restored through a process in which the parts receive their functions from the developing whole, and the whole exists only in their coordinated activity. The regenerated limb is no item retrieved intact from a catalogue. Nor does the organism's history alone explain it. The question is how the living whole determines what counts as damage, what counts as restoration, and how it can reconstitute the conditions of its own existence. That is the deeper experimental promise of Levin's biology. Beyond life, reflective agency introduces a further determination: a subject may examine and transform the ends it recognizes as its own. Neither repair nor freedom is captured by a mere increment on a scale of pattern complexity.

Levin is right to reject 'emergence' as a ceremonial name for ignorance, and to demand experiments that expose unexpected capacities. The same demand applies to his own ontology. Mathematical objectivity, unforeseen competence and a fruitful research programme establish no independent causal residence for form. If 'Platonic Space' names the objective articulation of possibilities, its scientific work can be preserved within the development of concrete systems. If it denotes an additional cause, its relation to embodiment and its contribution must be determined, rather than presupposed. Levin abstracts intelligible form from actuality, assigns the abstraction an independent address, and calls upon it to explain the actuality from which it was taken. What his experiments most vividly disclose is the richer truth he has thereby separated: a world whose forms are intelligible through the activity by which they become what they are.
