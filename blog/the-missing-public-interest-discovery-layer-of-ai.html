The Missing Public-Interest Discovery Layer of AI

How the systems through which AI finds tools are becoming a field of governance.

Petri Autio
Head of Product, mWater and Solstice · September 20, 2026

From a Prompt to an Operational System

Suppose a climate-change-induced flood hits a rural area hard, and a district officer wants to map out a response plan based on the latest satellite data and best available rapid flood-modelling tools as soon as possible. How can they achieve a good result quickly? Or take a district-wide programme to operationalise chlorine monitoring in all of the district’s schools in a low-resource region. What would be an effective way to go about this in today’s world?

For the first case, imagine that a person used to solving many daily problems with AI simply goes to their tool of choice and creates a good prompt. The AI agent could then understand the problem, ask for clarifications if needed, seek out up-to-date satellite data, fetch an open-source code repository containing a locally runnable flood-modelling tool, run it with the fresh inputs, produce a map and overlay it with water, sanitation and community information from a platform such as mWater. It would still need to surface uncertainties, check that the data and model are suitable for the local context, and leave consequential judgements with the responsible people. But the best available open-source data, academic science and ground-truthed infrastructure information could then be combined into a reviewable, actionable plan, complete with a prioritised list of response targets, in record time.

For the second, instead of the human user spending time assessing platforms and learning their ins and outs, an effective AI agent could discover the Solstice MCP server, find existing public data or ingest data from elsewhere, design surveys and workflows drawing on successful existing ones, customise them to the context, and propose user accounts and a suitable permissions structure for approval. It could then help the district focus on onboarding schools and making the process as frictionless as possible. Drawing on the solid data models of a well-established platform and its extensive capabilities, the district officer could build a whole operational management system in hours or days, while retaining responsibility for institutional decisions, data protection and the realities of adoption.

Google says that AI Overviews alone now reach more than 2.5 billion users each month, and the numbers continue to rise fast.1 The scenarios above will become increasingly feasible in the near-term future. But they depend on AIs knowing how to leverage existing platforms and their capabilities to their best effect, not to mention how to draw from the near-graveyard of fantastically capable but obscure open-source tools and inert data repositories.

How do we teach AI these things? When your Claude or Codex starts grasping for tools to help solve a problem in a crisis, when a flood is underway or landslides have just settled, how does it find a well-supported and contextually appropriate tool instead of settling for something subpar or confidently coding a less-than-ideal solution on the spot? More broadly, where does power sit as people become accustomed to having AIs and agents do their work for them?

Here I will argue that external communications should be self-consciously crafted with LLMs in mind, and that the tooling layer immediately above large language models is at risk of being neglected as a nexus of power and leverage for positive impact in humanitarian and development work. Ultimately, there should be transparently governed, authoritative registries of agentic skills for good governance and humanitarian response. Humanitarian and public-sector actors should treat the emerging infrastructure of agentic resources as a field of governance, not merely as a developer convenience.

The way we interact with software is changing, albeit at different speeds in different contexts and from one individual to another.

Making Good Work Legible to AI

If you have built a great flood-modelling tool, write its documentation so that it can be discovered and understood by AI. If you maintain a scalable software platform, it is better to overcommunicate than undercommunicate its capabilities: AIs can parse far more information than most prospective human users will ever read. An easy step is to publish an llms.txt file that points models and agentic tools which support the emerging convention towards the most useful, authoritative material on your site. It is a low-cost experiment, not a guarantee of indexing or preferential treatment. But when you write online, do not write only for humans. Write also for the averaged externalisation of human knowledge as it hums and buzzes around us through AIs.2

The next step is to make tools themselves discoverable. Agentic Resource Discovery, or ARD, is a draft open specification announced in June 2026 and developed with contributors from Microsoft, Google, Hugging Face and several other technology companies.3 It provides a common way to publish, index and search for resources such as tools, skills, APIs, MCP servers and specialised agents. Under the current draft, hosting a lightweight manifest at /.well-known/ard.json tells an AI what a resource does, who operates it, what authority it requires and how it can be invoked. Discovery services can then index these catalogues, allowing an agent to search for a capability when it needs one instead of requiring every possible tool to be installed in advance. Whether ARD itself achieves broad adoption remains uncertain, but the discovery problem it addresses is unlikely to disappear.4

For organisations such as Solstice and mWater that are widely used globally, including at government level, this creates an opportunity. They can publish catalogues that make their platforms and preferred methods of using them easy for agents to discover. Over time, they can also support registries that distinguish well-maintained, institutionally supported workflows from merely plausible-looking integrations. Good documentation tells an agent how a platform works; a catalogue helps the agent find that platform at the moment it is needed.

Let us think this further. Many good tools are announced at conferences, used during a funded project and then slowly forgotten as the funding ends and their creators move on. The minimum lift is to leave behind clear documentation, stable metadata and a machine-discoverable description of what the tool does, what inputs it needs and where its limitations lie. That alone gives useful work a better chance of being rediscovered. A small investment at the end of a project can extend its practical life far beyond the period in which someone is actively promoting it.

And let us keep pushing beyond individual organisations. Companies such as Google, Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic exert enormous influence through the models they train and the systems through which those models are distributed. The global public commons has limited ability to shape that layer directly. The next layer, however, namely the harnesses, catalogues, registries, permissions and selection mechanisms through which AI encounters the outside world, is still being actively formed. It is a layer at which governments, humanitarian organisations, universities and civil-society actors can have considerable influence if they engage early.

This extends beyond software tools to the broader discourse around subjects such as climate change. Organisations already invest in making evidence visible to policymakers, journalists and search engines. They must now also consider whether their most important findings, definitions and recommendations can be reliably found, interpreted and cited by AI systems. If authoritative institutions are absent or illegible in this environment, confident summaries built from weaker sources will fill the gap. The sector cannot afford to stand outside this conversation while its knowledge is increasingly mediated through these systems.

A Hundred Million Dollar Opportunity

Even a narrowly successful version could have substantial economic value. If a trusted discovery service saved professional time, prevented a small number of duplicated or failed digital investments, and improved the use of only one tenth of one percent of global development spending, its public value could reach roughly $250 million a year. At broader adoption, the value could plausibly exceed $1 billion annually, although most of that would appear as better public outcomes and avoided waste, not revenue to the registry itself.5

Discovery Is a Governance Question

When an AI seeks a tool for a given task, it should not simply be offered whichever private organisation has paid the highest promotional price. It should be able to find well-supported options suited to the job, taking account of evidence, local appropriateness, openness, security, maintenance and institutional trust. There is therefore a need for public-interest registries of agentic resources. These would not be merely inert data repositories like AWS Open Data, HDX, or Project W, waiting to be mined. They would be active force multipliers for an ever broader group of human users, giving many more people a pathway to become effective superusers.

The governance challenge is substantial. Someone must decide what is indexed, how resources are ranked, which claims are verified and how unsafe or abandoned tools are handled. Those decisions should not be hidden inside a proprietary recommendation system. A credible humanitarian or public-sector registry would need transparent criteria, plural governance, clear provenance and room for contextual judgement. No catalogue can determine the right tool independently of place, language, institutional capacity and risk. But it can make those factors visible and give both the agent and its human user better grounds for deciding.

In concrete terms, such a service could sit over open discovery standards and record verified publisher identity, maintenance status, evidence behind a tool’s claims, licences and data-governance constraints, languages and operating contexts, known failure modes, security assessments and experience from real deployments. It could expose its ranking criteria, distinguish publisher claims from independent assessments, log why a resource was recommended, and provide ways to correct or contest a listing. Its public value would lie not simply in storing entries, but in maintaining credible and revisable judgements about them.

There are, then, two broad ways to influence what large models do. One is to shape what they internalise during training and retrieve through ordinary search. This is slow, uncertain and largely controlled elsewhere, but organisations should still make their authoritative knowledge clear and machine-readable. The other is to shape the resources models can discover and use at runtime. That layer is more immediate, more open to intervention and, for operational organisations, potentially more consequential.

A public-interest registry alone would not ensure that commercial agents consult it or give its assessments meaningful weight. The political goal should therefore be broader: open and interoperable discovery, public-interest assurance and ranking, and procurement or institutional rules requiring public-sector agents to consult appropriate registries, disclose their sources and explain consequential selections. This would not remove the power of model and agent vendors, but it would make that power more visible and contestable.

Is there anything out there that could work as the institutional foundation? The Digital Public Goods Alliance maintains a registry of more than 250 recognised digital public goods, reassessed annually, and already exposes it through a public API.6 The Solstice Institute maintains libraries of public good indicators, workflows, environmental data sets, and more. A useful first push could add agentic profiles to twenty well-used tools, recording when they suit a task, where they fail and whether claims are publisher-asserted, independently verified or backed by a named deployment, then publish them through an ARD catalogue. Commercial marketplaces could index this assurance feed, while procurement guidance could require publicly funded agents to consult it and record why a tool was chosen. Extending an existing institution and standard would be a considerably shorter path than creating a new body and would give us something concrete to test.

What Organisations Can Do Now

  • Make documentation machine-readable: publish an llms.txt file at your site root to guide models towards authoritative datasets and methodology documents.

  • Expose tools via open specifications: if you have developed tools, APIs or agentic skills worth exposing to the wider world, experiment with publishing a /.well-known/ard.json manifest under the ARD specification.

  • Document workflows, not just features: detail when and in what context a tool should be deployed so agents can evaluate situational fit.

  • Champion public-interest registries: advocate for transparently governed registries within donor and humanitarian networks before commercial discovery marketplaces define ranking default rules.
